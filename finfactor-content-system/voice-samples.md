#POST1

Mutual Fund Central was a fix for a real problem. Wealth platforms needed a clean way to pull a customer's mutual fund holdings without scraping or screen-grabbing, and MFC delivered that.
The catch nobody talks about is that an MFC pull is a photograph. The customer who linked their portfolio on Tuesday looks different by Friday, and your cohort logic, your RM dashboard, and your cross-sell triggers are all reading Tuesday's version.
Pairing MFC with the AA layer is what closes this. The first pull captures the portfolio. The AA consent keeps it refreshing without asking the customer to relink anything.
If a wealth platform is running anything cohort-driven on top of MFC data, this gap is worth a look.

#POST2

The shape of MFI borrowing has shifted enough that the early-warning playbook most lenders are running is now reading the wrong inputs.

The change is on two axes. Average ticket sizes have moved up by around 27% over the past three years (Care Ratings), and the same customer now sits on the books of three or four lenders instead of one or two. The exposure per borrower has grown without the book growing proportionately.

Asset quality has caught up to this. Gross NPAs in the sector doubled in FY25, from 8.8% to roughly 16% (Brickwork Ratings).

Older monitoring systems were calibrated for the previous shape, where a stressed borrower could be absorbed because the rest of the cohort was healthy. That math gets harder when one default 
cascades across four lenders at once.

The MFIs spending less time on collections this quarter are the ones who stopped waiting for bureau updates and started reading cashflow directly.


#POST3
The April AMFI numbers are doing something interesting if you read them slowly. SIP inflows hit ₹31,115 crore, a record. The stoppage ratio stayed above 100% for the second month running.
Both numbers are real. They're describing two different customers.
The committed investor, 3-4 years in, kept increasing their contribution. The marginal investor, around month 12 or 14, walked away. AUM grew while the customer base thinned at the edges.
For a wealth platform this matters fast. The customers who cancelled in April were almost never the ones using anything else on the app. One SIP, set up at onboarding, ran on autopilot for a year, gone the first time the market got uncomfortable.
Of the SIPs that stopped in April, how many of those customers had logged in more than twice in the previous quarter? Our guess is very few. The engagement signal was sitting there for months.

#POST4
The Equirus Wealth report on women investors had a number worth sitting with. Not the headline (equity MF share went from 10% to 32%), but the behavioural data underneath it.
75 to 90 percent of these investors stayed invested through market corrections. Around 55 percent added capital during dips.
Given to a Head of Wealth blind, that profile reads as the ideal client. Long tenure, low servicing cost, unlikely to churn at the first wobble.
Worth pulling from your own book:
SIP stoppage rate among women investors versus the overall base.
Cohort acquired in the last 24 months versus the 24 before that.
The 32% is the easy story. Whether your acquisition funnel is built to compound it is the harder one.

#POST5
An ORF piece this week made a point that doesn't get said often enough in the AI-for-lending conversation. The same alternative data that lets a lender approve a thin-file borrower can also reject one, silently, with no explanation the borrower or the lender can easily reconstruct.
The article calls this "silent exclusion." For a CRO, it's a governance risk that hasn't been priced in yet.
If a rejected applicant asks why, the answer "the model said no" stops being acceptable the first time it shows up in a regulator query, a journalist's piece, or a class-action notice. RBI's digital lending norms don't yet require explainability. They will.
This is the same problem on the monitoring side. An early-warning signal is only useful to a CRO if they can point to what triggered it — a salary credit that moved, a current account balance that dropped, a second loan taken at a higher rate.
